# J.C.R. Licklider: The Man Who Dreamed the Internet Before It Existed

**Track P — Pioneers | Episode 01**
*Wired: A History of the Internet*

---

Sometime around 1957, a psychologist in his early forties sat down to do something he later described, with the mild embarrassment of a man catching himself in a wasteful habit, as a "little study" of his own working day. Joseph Carl Robnett Licklider — known to nearly everyone who worked with him simply as "Lick" — tracked how he actually spent his research time, expecting to find long stretches devoted to genuine intellectual work: forming hypotheses, weighing evidence, arriving at insights. What he found instead horrified him. Roughly eighty-five percent of his time went to what he called "getting into a position to think" — locating a reference, plotting a graph, transcribing data, the accumulated clerical friction that had to be cleared away before any actual thinking could begin. Only the remaining sliver of his day was spent doing the thing he had actually trained for.

That small, self-administered experiment in time-use accounting would turn out to be one of the more consequential personal audits in the history of computing. It convinced Licklider that the relationship between humans and machines was arranged backward — that computers, still widely regarded in the late 1950s as glorified calculators fit only for batch-processing punch cards overnight, could instead absorb exactly the kind of routine, mechanical labor that was eating his own research life alive, freeing him and everyone like him to spend their time on judgment, creativity, and the formulation of good questions rather than the clerical work of answering them. He would spend the following decade developing that insight into a body of ideas, and then, in a stroke of circumstance he could not have fully anticipated, into an extraordinary amount of federal research funding — money that would go on to seed nearly every foundational technology this Chronicle has traced across its preceding episodes, including the ARPANET itself. Licklider never wrote a line of ARPANET's code and never built a working network of his own. He is, nonetheless, one of the two or three people without whom this entire series would have nothing to chronicle at all.

## From Psychoacoustics to a Religious Conversion

Licklider's path to computing ran through a discipline that, on its face, had little to do with either psychology or machines: the physiology of hearing. Born March 11, 1915, in St. Louis, Missouri, the only child of a Baptist minister, Licklider earned a triple bachelor's degree at Washington University in St. Louis, majoring in physics, mathematics, and psychology, before completing a doctorate in 1942 at the University of Rochester. He lectured at Harvard through the 1940s before moving to MIT in 1950, where he became involved in a project studying how human operators would interact with SAGE, a proposed computerized air-defense system designed to track incoming aircraft in real time. Working through how a person could usefully monitor a continuous stream of fast-moving radar data gave Licklider his first sustained exposure to the idea that a computer might function not as a distant batch-processing tool but as something a person interacted with directly, in the same moment the thinking was happening. He left MIT in 1957 for Bolt Beranek and Newman, an acoustics firm — the same company that would, a decade later, build the Interface Message Processors chronicled in this Chronicle's third episode — specifically because BBN offered him room to pursue computing alongside his acoustics work. It was there, working with an early minicomputer, that Licklider experienced what he called his "religious conversion to interactive computing."

## The Fig Wasp and the Fig Tree

Licklider's conversion produced, in March 1960, the single document most responsible for his lasting reputation: a paper titled "Man-Computer Symbiosis," published in the IRE Transactions on Human Factors in Electronics. It opened with an image few readers would have expected from a piece of computer-science writing: the fig tree, which can be pollinated only by a specific insect, the fig wasp, whose own larva depends entirely on the fig tree's ovary for food. Neither organism can complete its life cycle without the other — the biological definition of symbiosis, and the concept Licklider proposed extending to the relationship between human beings and computing machines.

The vision Licklider laid out was more measured than either the utopianism or the anxious replacement narratives dominating contemporary discussion of computing's future. He was explicit that he was not describing artificial intelligence in the sense of machines eventually thinking independently of human direction: "Men will set the goals, formulate the hypotheses, determine the criteria, and perform the evaluations," he wrote. "Computing machines will do the routinizable work that must be done to prepare the way for insights and decisions." He simply conceded "dominance in the distant future of cerebration to machines alone" without much apparent concern, since his interest lay in the nearer-term question of how humans and computers might work together productively — an approach later given its own name, "intelligence amplification," distinguishing it from the more autonomy-focused ambitions of artificial intelligence research proper.

The paper also introduced a concept Licklider called the "thinking center," a facility combining the functions of a library with networked information technology, accessible to users at various remote locations simultaneously — an idea he developed further in a 1965 study for the Council on Library Resources, and one that reads, with six decades of hindsight, as a strikingly accurate sketch of what a data center or cloud computing platform would eventually become.

## An Unlikely Appointment at the Pentagon

Licklider's ideas might well have remained a well-regarded but academic contribution to computing's history had he not been offered, in 1962, the directorship of a newly created office within ARPA: the Information Processing Techniques Office, or IPTO. The office had originally been conceived under a narrower military name, Command and Control Research, and one of Licklider's first acts was to rename it — a small gesture signaling a real shift away from a narrowly defined military mission and toward the broader computing research questions he actually wanted to fund.

Across the roughly two years Licklider ran IPTO, he directed funds toward seeding time-sharing research and computer science education at a small number of universities he judged most likely to produce foundational advances — MIT, UC Berkeley, Carnegie Mellon, and Stanford prominent among them — funding that helped establish some of the first formal computer science departments in the country and that would, within a few years, produce the very researchers the ARPANET's initial nodes would connect. He funded Project MAC at MIT and provided early support to Douglas Engelbart's Augmentation Research Center at Stanford Research Institute — the lab that would go on to develop the computer mouse and demonstrate, in 1968's celebrated "Mother of All Demos," many of the interactive computing concepts Licklider's own paper had sketched in the abstract. Established computer manufacturers criticized this approach at the time, arguing that time-sharing was an inefficient use of expensive machine resources; Licklider persisted regardless.

## The Intergalactic Computer Network

Licklider's most direct conceptual contribution to what would become the internet arrived not in a formal paper but in an internal ARPA memorandum, circulated in 1962 and again more famously in April 1963, addressed with playful grandiosity to the "Members and Affiliates of the Intergalactic Computer Network." The memo proposed building connective infrastructure that would let ARPA's scattered, individually funded researchers and institutions draw on and reinforce one another rather than proceeding in isolation.

Licklider left ARPA in 1964, several years before any of this networking vision took concrete technical shape, and did not personally participate in the specific engineering work chronicled in this Chronicle's earlier episodes on packet switching and the ARPANET's first message. But the two men most directly responsible for actually building that network — Robert Taylor, who would go on to fund the ARPANET proposal as a subsequent IPTO director, and Lawrence Roberts, the ARPA program manager who oversaw its construction — both worked within the framework Licklider had established, at institutions Licklider himself had helped select. Taylor, who later founded Xerox PARC's Computer Science Laboratory, put the debt plainly: "Most of the significant advances in computer technology — including the work that my group did at Xerox PARC — were simply extrapolations of Lick's vision. They were not really new visions of their own. So he was really the father of it all."

## A Man Who Preferred Not to Take Credit

By most contemporary accounts, Licklider's own temperament sat oddly alongside the magnitude of his influence. Colleagues consistently described him as unusually modest, with a habit of directing humor at his own expense, and a personal style that made him, in the words of one memorial tribute, "the most unlikely 'great man' you could ever encounter." He returned briefly to government service as IPTO's director once more from 1974 to 1975, and remained an active presence in computing research through the 1970s and 1980s, including an unusually prescient 1967 paper for the Carnegie Commission on Educational Television arguing for a two-way, networked alternative to the traditional broadcast model — a vision that, like so much of his earlier work, arrived decades ahead of the infrastructure that would eventually make it practical.

Licklider died on June 26, 1990, in Arlington, Massachusetts, roughly six months before the ARPANET itself was formally decommissioned, chronicled in this Chronicle's sixth episode, and just as the World Wide Web he never directly worked on but had, in a real sense, spent thirty years anticipating was beginning its earliest, still-private existence at CERN. He left behind no company, no patent, and no single piece of enduring code. What he left instead was a founding conviction, formed from nothing more dramatic than an honest accounting of his own wasted afternoons, that computers were at their most valuable not as replacements for human judgment but as partners in it — and the funding decisions, made across barely two years running a small Pentagon research office, that gave the people who would go on to build the actual network the money, the mandate, and the intellectual permission to try.
