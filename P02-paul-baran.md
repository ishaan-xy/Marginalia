# Paul Baran: The Stubborn Engineer Who Invented Packet Switching

**Track P — Pioneers | Episode 02**
*Wired: A History of the Internet*

---

Before he was Paul Baran, he was Pesach Baran, the youngest of three children born to a Jewish family in Grodno, Poland, on April 29, 1926. His family emigrated to the United States when he was two, settling first in Boston and then in Philadelphia, where his father, Morris, opened a small grocery store. Young Paul spent much of his childhood helping with deliveries, pulling a little red wagon loaded with groceries through the neighborhood streets — an unglamorous apprenticeship in reliability and persistence that, in the retrospective accounts colleagues later offered of his character, seems to have left a permanent mark. This Chronicle's earlier episodes have already told the technical story of what Baran built: a distributed, packet-switched network design that the American telephone industry dismissed as unworkable, and that would go on to shape the internet's fundamental architecture regardless. This profile turns instead to the man who built it, and to the considerably longer career he spent afterward, mostly outside the spotlight his central idea eventually earned him.

## From Grocery Deliveries to UNIVAC

Baran's formal path into engineering was straightforward by the standards of his generation: a degree in electrical engineering from Drexel Institute of Technology, completed in 1949. His first job placed him closer to the actual birth of computing than almost any young engineer could have hoped for — a technician's position at the Eckert-Mauchly Computer Corporation, working on the UNIVAC, the first commercially produced computer in the United States, built by the same team that had earlier developed ENIAC. He moved from there to the Raymond Rosen Engineering Products Company, where he worked as a field engineer on the earliest telemetry systems at Cape Canaveral, and then, in 1955, to Hughes Aircraft in Los Angeles, doing radar data processing after marrying Evelyn Murphy that same year. While working full-time at Hughes, Baran attended night classes at UCLA, completing a master's degree in engineering in 1959 under advisor Gerald Estrin, with a thesis on character recognition — a detail his advisor later singled out as revealing something distinctive about Baran even then: he was, in Estrin's recollection, the first student he had supervised who took the trouble to investigate whether his own thesis work might be patentable, a sign that Baran was already thinking like a practical inventor rather than purely an academic researcher.

It was this same year, 1959, that Baran joined the RAND Corporation's mathematics division, where he would spend the next nine years — and where, within roughly three years of arriving, he would develop the survivable-network concepts chronicled in detail in this Chronicle's first two episodes. What those earlier episodes could only gesture toward, given their focus on the technical substance of Baran's proposal, is the sheer institutional stubbornness the work actually required. Baran's design ran directly against the assumptions of an entire telephone industry, and the RAND report series documenting it, "On Distributed Communications," published in full in 1964, took years to write and considerably longer to be taken seriously by anyone positioned to build it.

## An Engineer Who Kept Working Regardless

What distinguishes Baran's biography from a simple story of vindicated genius is what he did after the Air Force's rejection and the subsequent institutional reshuffling that buried his proposal, chronicled in this Chronicle's second episode. Baran did not abandon networking research in frustration; he simply kept working, at RAND and afterward, on a strikingly varied set of problems that had little to do with the packet-switching concept that would eventually make him famous. While still at RAND, he designed one of the first doorway gun detectors, an early metal-detection security device, and in 1965 became the first computer scientist to testify before the United States Congress on the emerging problem of computer privacy — a concern that placed him, remarkably, decades ahead of the privacy debates this Chronicle's later episodes on surveillance capitalism and data protection would go on to chronicle in far more contentious form.

Baran left RAND in 1968 to co-found the Institute for the Future, a nonprofit research organization dedicated to long-range forecasting methodology — a fitting next step for a man who had already spent nearly a decade thinking several decades ahead of his industry's actual technical capacity. He continued to serve as an informal consultant to the ARPANET project even after this departure, a quiet, ongoing involvement in a network being built, at Lawrence Roberts's direction, substantially on the strength of Baran's own earlier, once-dismissed reports.

## Six Companies, One Recurring Pattern

Baran's post-RAND career, largely overshadowed in most popular retellings of internet history by the singular packet-switching story, was in fact remarkably prolific by any ordinary measure of entrepreneurial output. Between the early 1970s and his death, Baran founded or co-founded six separate companies in Silicon Valley, several of which were acquired by larger firms once their underlying technology proved commercially significant. Cabledata Associates, established in Menlo Park in 1974, became something of an incubator for further spin-off ventures: Equatorial Communications, an early satellite-communications company later absorbed into GTE; Telebit, built around a high-speed modem technology Baran himself had invented, eventually acquired by Cisco Systems in 1996; and Packet Technologies, which evolved into StrataCom, a fast packet-switching company for voice and data that Cisco would also go on to acquire. In 1985, Baran co-founded Metricom alongside the Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen, building an early wireless mesh networking system called Ricochet that anticipated, if somewhat ahead of the market it needed, the wireless internet access that would become commonplace only years later. Baran founded a sixth company, Com21, in 1992, which grew into Europe's largest supplier of cable modem systems, with Baran serving as its board chairman until his death.

The recurring pattern across all six ventures is worth noting on its own terms: Baran did not simply enjoy one lucky, era-defining insight early in his career and coast on its reputation afterward. He kept building genuinely new communications technologies — satellite links, high-speed modems, wireless mesh networks, cable infrastructure — each one arriving, characteristically, somewhat ahead of the market's readiness to fully embrace it, in a pattern that echoed the packet-switching proposal itself: technically sound, commercially difficult to place, and eventually vindicated once the surrounding technological and market conditions caught up to what Baran had already anticipated. In 1971, well before email had become the dominant form of network communication this Chronicle's fourth episode described, Baran was already predicting the eventual arrival of household email — one more instance of an unusually consistent talent for seeing around technological corners years before anyone else found the view worth remarking on.

## A Reputation That Arrived Late, and Was Handled Modestly

Recognition for Baran's foundational contribution to networking accumulated gradually and, by most accounts, later than the scale of his actual contribution might have warranted. He received the IEEE Alexander Graham Bell Medal in 1990, three decades after his original RAND reports, and the Marconi Prize the following year, along with eventual induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. Paul Saffo, a longtime colleague, offered an observation about Baran's professional temperament that helps explain why broader public recognition took as long as it did to arrive: Baran believed innovation was fundamentally "a team process," and consistently avoided the kind of individual credit-seeking that might have accelerated his own reputation, preferring instead to let the work itself, and the people who built on it, carry whatever recognition eventually followed.

Baran died at his home in Palo Alto on March 26, 2011, at the age of eighty-four, from complications of lung cancer. The tributes that followed from figures central to this Chronicle's own account of the internet's founding captured something of both the technical debt and the personal regard he had earned. Vinton Cerf, whose own work on TCP/IP this Chronicle examined in its fifth episode, remarked that "Paul wasn't afraid to go in directions counter to what everyone else thought was the right or only thing to do." Robert Kahn, Cerf's co-architect on that same protocol suite, called Baran "one of the finest gentlemen I ever met and creative to the very end." RAND's own president at the time, James Thomson, offered perhaps the most fitting summary available: "Our world is a better place for the technologies Paul Baran invented and developed, and also because of his consistent concern with appropriate public policies for their use." It is a legacy built less on a single triumphant vindication than on nearly five decades of quiet, persistent, frequently premature invention — a career-long habit, formed perhaps as early as those childhood grocery deliveries, of simply doing the reliable work in front of him and trusting that the value of it would eventually be recognized, whether or not that recognition arrived on any particular schedule he could control.
